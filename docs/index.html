Diwali night is the spike. The season is the smog.

Every autumn Delhi's air turns grey, and every year Diwali gets the blame. We measured how much of the October–November rise in fine-particle pollution (PM2.5) comes from Diwali night, and how much from the season itself, using hourly data from 11 government monitoring stations over three Diwalis (2023, 2024, 2025).

Introduction

Delhi's air quality collapses every October and November. Two explanations dominate the news: Diwali fireworks and the season, meaning colder, stiller air that traps pollution near the ground, plus smoke from crop-residue (stubble) burning in Punjab and Haryana.

Our question: how much of the October–November PM2.5 rise in Delhi comes from Diwali night, and how much from the season's weather?

PM2.5 means particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres, small enough to reach deep into the lungs. It is measured in micrograms per cubic metre (µg/m³). India's 24-hour standard is 60 µg/m³; the WHO guideline is 15.

Why two PM2.5 sources? The project brief names OpenAQ, but OpenAQ holds no Delhi government-station data between late 2022 and February 2025, so only the 2025 Diwali was there. We went to the original publisher, CPCB, for all three years and kept OpenAQ for 2025 as a cross-check.

Study window: 15 September to 30 November each year. Late September is the "before the season" baseline. Diwali night means 18:00 on Diwali day to 06:00 the next morning.

Cleaning, in brief

  • Formats: renamed columns with units in their names, parsed text dates, converted OpenAQ's UTC-offset times to IST and its 15-minute readings to hourly means, and converted station wind from m/s to km/h.
  • Duplicates across sources: matched each CPCB station to its OpenAQ twin by comparing values hour by hour, because names differ ("Pusa, Delhi - IMD" vs "Pusa, Delhi - IITM"). Then dropped 20,120 duplicate OpenAQ hours.
  • Same site, two names: merged the two Pusa monitors (same coordinates) into one site, leaving 11.
  • Outliers: removed 55 impossible values (zero or negative), 43 hours of stuck sensors and 28 isolated single-station spikes. Kept 154 extreme hours that many stations recorded at once, such as Diwali nights; they are real.
  • Missing values: under 3% of hours. They are linked to station and season, not to how dirty the air was (MAR). Gaps of up to 3 hours were interpolated (482 hours); longer gaps were left empty (1,487 hours, 2.4%).

Every step, with the number of rows affected, is in the cleaning log. The full code is in the three notebooks.

How we answer the question

  1. Seasonal rise: city-wide average PM2.5 in October–November minus the 15–30 September average.
  2. Weather's part: two simple regression models of daily PM2.5 on weather. A ventilation-only model (mixing height, wind) gives a lower estimate. A full-weather model (adding temperature and humidity) gives an upper estimate. We then ask how much rise each predicts from the weather alone.
  3. Diwali night's part: Diwali-night PM2.5 minus (a) the nights 2–7 days either side, and (b) what that night's weather predicts. We spread the 12-hour excess over the 1,464 hours of October–November to get its share of the seasonal rise.

Results

Conclusion

Diwali night accounts for about 1–4% of Delhi's October–November PM2.5 rise; the other 96–99% comes with the season.

Diwali night is the single worst event of the autumn: city-wide PM2.5 reached 679, 650 and 1,331 µg/m³ in 2023, 2024 and 2025, and the night averaged 1.8 to 8.4 times what the weather predicted. But it lasts about 12 hours, while the seasonal rise of roughly 130 µg/m³ lasts two months. Weather explains between a small share (ventilation alone, 3–41%) and nearly all (with temperature, 72–122%) of that seasonal rise. Our data cannot separate colder, stiller air from the stubble smoke and heating that arrive at the same time of year.

In plain words: banning fireworks would remove the worst night of the year, which matters for anyone breathing that night, but it would barely change the October–November average. The season, not the festival, is what keeps Delhi's air above the safe limit for two months.

Limitations

  • No stubble-burning data. We did not measure crop fires, so "the season" mixes weather, stubble smoke, heating and traffic. The weather share is a range, not one number.
  • Weather from a model, at one point. Open-Meteo is modelled weather on a grid several kilometres wide, used for the whole city. Station sensors broadly agree on temperature (correlation 0.87), but their wind readings sit on a floor value 40% of the time, so they could not replace it.
  • Small number of Diwalis. Three nights cannot give a precise average effect; 2025 behaves very differently from 2023 and 2024.
  • Possible capping before 2025. No reading in 2023–2024 exceeds about 1,000 µg/m³, but nine stations went above it at once in 2025. Older peaks may have been capped by the instruments, which would make the 2023–2024 Diwali excess an underestimate.
  • Station choice. 12 of the 59 Delhi stations listed by CPCB, chosen to cover all parts of the city (11 sites after merging the two Pusa monitors). A different set could shift city averages slightly.
  • Association, not cause. The regressions show which weather goes with dirtier air; they do not prove causes.
